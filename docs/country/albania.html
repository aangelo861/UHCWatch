How many households face financial hardship?

In 2015, 12.5% of households had catastrophic health spending. In the poorest fifth of households it was 40.7%; in the richest fifth, 0.6%. Among households with catastrophic spending, medicines accounted for the largest share of out-of-pocket payments (64.8%). 8.2% of households were impoverished or further impoverished by out-of-pocket payments (2015).

Who goes without care?

In 2023, 10.3% of people had unmet need for health care: 16.6% in the poorest fifth and 4.6% in the richest. In 2023, 12.5% of people had unmet need for dental care: 21.0% in the poorest fifth and 7.7% in the richest.

How much does government spend on health?

Public spending on health was 3.5% of GDP in 2023. The median across 53 countries is 5.5%. Health took 12.3% of government spending (2023).

Where are the gaps in coverage?

Main gaps in population coverage

The Mandatory Health Insurance Fund (MHIF) only covers about two thirds of the population on average and only about half of those in the poorest fifth of the population. People not covered by the MHIF tend to be informal workers, people with low incomes, those from minority communities (Roma and Egyptian people) and people living in deprived areas. Non-covered people are only entitled to publicly financed emergency care, an annual basic screening and counselling health check-up (since 2015 and for people aged 35-70 years only) and primary care visits (since 2017). They also face difficulties in using the e-Albania portal, which limits their access to health care.

Main gaps in service coverage

Although the publicly financed benefits package is relatively comprehensive, the range of covered medicines remains low by European standards, there is no entitlement to dental care for adults and entitlement to dental care for children is limited. Informal payments are widespread for services and supplies that should be available free of charge, mainly in inpatient care.

Main gaps caused by user charges

User charges in the form of percentage co-payments are applied to outpatient prescribed medicines and diagnostic tests and other paramedical services (e.g. physiotherapy, counselling). Protection from co-payments is weak: there is no overall cap on co-payments and, although exemptions are available for some groups of people and some services, they do not target people with low incomes. There are no formal user charges for inpatient medicines, but people report having to purchase their own medicines in hospitals. The complexity of co-payment policy lowers transparency and undermines public trust in the health system.

VHI role in filling gaps in coverage

VHI plays a very small supplementary role in the health system, covering access to health care in private facilities (mainly hospitals). In 2020 it accounted for 0% of current spending on health. Expanding VHI is likely to increase inequality in access to health care.

Source: UHC watch policy explorer.

What does UHC Watch recommend?

UHC Watch’s recommendations for improving financial protection in Albania, in its own words.

De-link entitlement to publicly financed health services from payment of contributions for the whole population

Albania should start by extending Mandatory Health Insurance Fund (MHIF) benefits to the whole population so that access no longer depends on payment of contributions. Linking entitlement to payment of MHIF contributions is particularly challenging given Albania’s large informal sector.

Simplify the complex design of user charges and strengthen protection mechanisms

User charges are complex and particularly heavy for outpatient prescribed medicines. Co-payment policy should be re-designed in the following ways: replace percentage co-payments with low, fixed co-payments; exempt households with low incomes from all co-payments; introduce an annual income-related cap covering all co-payments.

Strengthen medicines policy

Attention should be paid to the way in which medicines are selected for coverage and to ensuring that physicians, pharmacists and people are able to prescribe, dispense and use the cheapest alternatives.

Increase public spending on health and use resources to meet equity and efficiency goals

Strengthening financial protection will require additional public spending on health and a greater focus on improving affordable access to health care for households with low incomes.

Source: UHC watch policy explorer and Can people afford to pay for health care? New evidence on financial protection in Albania (2020).
