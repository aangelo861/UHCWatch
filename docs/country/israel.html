How many households face financial hardship?

In 2022, 5.4% of households had catastrophic health spending. In the poorest fifth of households it was 15.5%; in the richest fifth, 2.5%. Among households with catastrophic spending, dental care accounted for the largest share of out-of-pocket payments (60.0%). 2.3% of households were impoverished or further impoverished by out-of-pocket payments (2022).

Where are the gaps in coverage?

Main gaps in population coverage

Undocumented migrants and most non-working refugees and asylum seekers have very limited access to publicly financed health care: emergency care and some primary care and specialist care for selected conditions only. If they are employed, they are required to be covered by mandatory private health insurance purchased by employers and jointly funded by employers (who must pay at least two thirds of the flat-rate premium cost) and employees. However, insurers offering this form of coverage are not required to cover pre-existing conditions or childbirth and it is difficult to enforce mandatory private health insurance for undocumented migrants. The children (< 18) of undocumented migrants and children (< 18) without a visa can only be covered if their parents pay a health contribution and they lose their coverage if their parents fail to pay it for more than 6 months.

Main gaps in service coverage

The National Health Insurance scheme offers only limited coverage of dental care for adults aged between 19 and 72, glasses (only covered for children < 8), psychotherapy and long-term care at home. Waiting times are an issue for publicly financed elective surgical procedures, specialist outpatient care, imaging diagnostic tests, rehabilitation and child development care.

Main gaps caused by user charges

Primary care visits, visits to some specialists (paediatrics, gynaecology and internal medicine) and inpatient care are free at the point of use but user charges are applied to all other outpatient health care and the design of these co-payments is complex. Percentage co-payments are applied to outpatient prescribed medicines and although there is a cap on these co-payments for people with chronic conditions, there are no exemptions for people with low incomes. All households benefit from a separate cap on co-payments for outpatient specialist visits and diagnostic tests, but there is no overall cap on all user charges. Neither of the caps is linked to income and income-based exemptions are usually only available for people > 65.

VHI role in filling gaps in coverage

VHI plays a complementary role, covering services excluded from or not so well covered by the NHI scheme (e.g. dental care, child development services and occupational therapy), and a supplementary role, providing people with faster access to and greater choice of private treatment (including in public hospitals). Although VHI covers over 80% of the population and accounts for a relatively high share of current spending on health (12% in 2023), it presents several challenges for equity and efficiency: take-up of VHI is much higher in richer than poorer households; VHI premiums represent an additional financial burden on households; VHI does not address gaps caused by user charges; and it plays a very limited role in addressing gaps in dental care coverage. As a result, VHI exacerbates inequalities in access to health care and undermines efficiency by skewing health care resources towards richer households and drawing health care professionals away from publicly financed providers.

Source: UHC watch policy explorer.

What does UHC Watch recommend?

UHC Watch’s recommendations for improving financial protection in Israel, in its own words.

Expand coverage of publicly financed dental care

Improve National Health Insurance (NHI) coverage of dental care, particularly for children and adults with low incomes.

Improve co-payment policy

Reduce co-payments (discounts) for people receiving welfare support from the National Insurance Institute as a first step towards exempting people with low incomes from all co-payments; unify existing co-payment caps by extending them to all co-payments and linking them to income, so that they are more protective for people with lower incomes; and replace percentage co-payments for medicines with low, fixed (flat-rate) co-payments.

Strengthen population coverage for non-permanent residents

Improve access to coverage for non-permanent resident children by breaking the link between their entitlement to publicly financed health care and payment of contributions by their parents, and extend coverage for non-permanent resident adults who are not employed.

Reduce the need for VHI

The Government should find ways to curb the growth of VHI providing faster access to care and limit dual coverage (holding VHI from a health plan and a commercial insurer). Israel’s experience, echoed by international experience, shows that when this type of VHI is allowed to play a significant role in the health system, it undermines equity and efficiency by exacerbating gaps in access to care based on income and VHI ownership. Part of the solution lies in bringing down waiting times for publicly financed health care to more acceptable levels, finding ways to prevent doctors from shifting users from public to private practice and improving access to health care in peripheral areas.

Sustain and increase public spending on health

Public spending on health should rise as a share of the government budget and as a share of GDP so that it is in line with Israel’s level of economic development. Additional funds can be used to reduce financial hardship and unmet need in the ways set out above.

Source: 'Can people afford to pay for health care? New evidence on financial protection in Israel' (in press).
