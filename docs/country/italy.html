How many households face financial hardship?

In 2022, 8.6% of households had catastrophic health spending. In the poorest fifth of households it was 27.5%; in the richest fifth, 1.7%. Among households with catastrophic spending, dental care accounted for the largest share of out-of-pocket payments (39.8%). 3.7% of households were impoverished or further impoverished by out-of-pocket payments (2022).

Who goes without care?

In 2025, 2.5% of people had unmet need for health care: 4.4% in the poorest fifth and 0.8% in the richest. In 2025, 2.9% of people had unmet need for dental care: 6.0% in the poorest fifth and 0.7% in the richest. 5.8% of people needing care could not afford a prescribed medicine (2019).

How much do people pay out of pocket?

Out-of-pocket payments were 22.3% of current spending on health in 2024. The median across 53 countries is 22.0%. Households spent 5.5% of their budget on out-of-pocket payments for health care (2022).

How much does government spend on health?

Public spending on health was 6.3% of GDP in 2024. The median across 53 countries is 5.5%. Health took 11.4% of government spending (2023).

Where are the gaps in coverage?

Main gaps in population coverage

Entitlement to publicly financed health care is based on residence, which means all registered residents (including refugees and asylum seekers) are automatically covered. Undocumented adults only have access to publicly financed emergency care, public health programmes and maternity care. A recent report indicates that about 500 000 undocumented migrants live in Italy.

Main gaps in service coverage

The publicly financed benefits package does not cover most dental care for adults. Coverage of dental care for children and medical products is limited.

Procedures and deadlines for updating the benefits package (the Livelli Essenziali di Assistenza in Italian, or the LEA) are specified in law but rarely followed, so the benefits package is not regularly updated.

Waiting times are a major issue for non-urgent inpatient and outpatient specialist care and diagnostic services.

There is significant inequality in access to quality health care between regions in the north and the south of the country. Health systems in more deprived regions tend to perform less well than those in richer regions.

Main gaps caused by user charges

User charges (co-payments) vary across regions. All regions apply co-payments to medical products, outpatient specialist visits, diagnostic tests and dental care. For outpatient prescribed medicines, all regions apply reference pricing (“avoidable co-payments”) and 12 out of 21 NHS regions also apply fixed co-payments.

Although there are exemptions in place for several groups of people with low incomes, including very young children, pensioners and unemployed people, there are none for workers with low incomes or to mitigate “avoidable co-payments” caused by reference pricing and there is no overall cap on co-payments.

People can claim an annual tax refund equal to 19% of any out-of-pocket payments above €129 but this policy favours people with higher incomes.

VHI role in filling gaps in coverage

VHI plays a supplementary role (offering faster access to treatment and greater choice of provider) and a complementary role (covering user charges and excluded services) and is provided by non-profit ‘health funds’ and commercial (for-profit) insurers. In 2023 VHI accounted for 2% of current spending on health. Expanding VHI is likely to increase inequality in access to health care.

What does UHC Watch recommend?

UHC Watch’s recommendations for improving financial protection in Italy, in its own words.

Address long waiting times for outpatient specialist visits and diagnostic tests

Although reducing waiting times is a high priority on the political agenda, further efforts should aim to expand capacity through additional financial resources and enhanced productivity, including reducing inappropriate use of care by establishing clear clinical guidelines for health care providers.

Improve the design of co-payments, especially for households with low incomes, and reduce regional variation

Current co-payment exemptions should be extended to all working-aged people with low incomes and to co-payments for outpatient prescribed medicines (including the “avoidable co-payments” caused by reference pricing). The Government should also introduce a cap on all co-payments for the whole population – ideally, the cap would be linked to household income so that it is more protective for people with lower incomes and has less impact on the NHS budget.

Reduce “avoidable co-payments” for more expensive medicines when cheaper alternatives are available

This can be achieved by encouraging greater use of generics through stronger prescribing and dispensing policies; educational campaigns for users; and extending protection from co-payments (e.g. exemptions) to “avoidable co-payments” caused by reference pricing.

Review the tax rebate on out-of-pocket payments

Review the equity and efficiency of the 19% tax rebate on out-of-pocket payments, which tends to favour people with higher incomes.

Expand NHS coverage of dental care and medical products

Expand the benefits package for dental care and medical products, including hearing aids and optical care, particularly for people with low incomes.

Address regional inequalities in access to health care

Options include improving care quality in the worst-performing regions and covering travel costs for people with low incomes who need highly specialized care in other regions (especially for cancer and rare diseases).

Close gaps in population coverage

This can be done by extending entitlement to NHS benefits to adult undocumented migrants, reducing administrative barriers to entitlements and better monitoring. Countries like Spain and France offer examples of good practice in extending coverage to adult undocumented migrants.

Ensure that levels of public spending on health are adequate to meet population health needs

To meet equity and efficiency goals now and in the future, particularly in the context of population ageing, the Government should ensure that levels of public spending on health are sufficient and carefully targeted to reduce financial hardship and unmet need for households with low incomes. It should also find ways to improve equity across regions.

Source: ‘Can people afford to pay for health care? New evidence on financial protection in Italy' (2025). See the coverage policy checklist for country examples of good practice in improving financial protection.
