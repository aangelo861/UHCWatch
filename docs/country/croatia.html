Croatia page

How many households face financial hardship?

In 2019, 3.6% of households had catastrophic health spending. In the poorest fifth of households it was 13.9%; in the richest fifth, 2.3%. Among households with catastrophic spending, dental care accounted for the largest share of out-of-pocket payments (49.5%). 1.9% of households were impoverished or further impoverished by out-of-pocket payments (2019).

Who goes without care?

In 2024, 1.3% of people had unmet need for health care: 3.5% in the poorest fifth and 0.3% in the richest. In 2024, 0.5% of people had unmet need for dental care: 1.3% in the poorest fifth and 0.2% in the richest. 10.7% of people needing care could not afford a prescribed medicine (2019).

How much do people pay out of pocket?

Out-of-pocket payments were 9.4% of current spending on health in 2023. The median across 53 countries is 22.0%. Households spent 3.1% of their budget on out-of-pocket payments for health care (2019).

How much does government spend on health?

Public spending on health was 6.1% of GDP in 2023. The median across 53 countries is 5.5%. Health took 12.8% of government spending (2023).

Where are the gaps in coverage?

Main gaps in population coverage

Undocumented migrants, asylum seekers and non-resident foreigners from countries outside the European Union are not entitled to mandatory health insurance benefits.

Main gaps in service coverage

Waiting times are an issue, but there are no waiting time guarantees in place. Informal payments play a small role in the health system.

Main gaps caused by user charges

User charges are applied to all health services except emergency care. People with low incomes are not exempt from co-payments but can apply for free complementary VHI covering co-payments. The cap on co-payments is set at a high level and only applies per episode of care, not over time. It does not apply to co-payments for outpatient prescribed medicines.

VHI role in filling gaps in coverage

Around 80% of the population has complementary VHI covering co-payments. This type of VHI is mainly supplied by the Croatian Institute of Health Insurance (HZZO) but also by private insurers. A quarter of those with complementary VHI supplied by the HZZO benefit from free access to VHI: the Government pays premiums for students, people with low incomes, people with severe disabilities and blood and organ donors. Despite this subsidy, households in the poorest fifth of the population spend a higher share of their budget on VHI than those in the richest fifth. VHI also plays a minor supplementary role, giving 8% of the population access to private providers, mainly for diagnostic services. In 2020 VHI accounted for 5.3% of current spending on health.

Source: UHC watch policy explorer.

What does UHC Watch recommend?

UHC Watch’s recommendations for improving financial protection in Croatia, in its own words.

Improve access to complementary VHI for households with low incomes

Raising the income threshold for eligibility for free complementary VHI would ensure that all households in the poorest fifth of the population are able to benefit. This would also help to address the regressivity of VHI premiums.

Strengthen protection from co-payments through better design

This can be done by extending the current list of exemptions from co-payments to include low-income households and by improving the cap on co-payments – for example, by applying it to all co-payments and ensuring it provides protection over time rather than per episode of care.

Improve the affordability of non-covered medicines

The lack of price regulation for non-covered medicines, including over-the-counter medicines, may be an issue, especially since medicines account for the bulk of out-of-pocket payments and catastrophic spending on health.

Source: ‘Can people afford to pay for health care? New evidence on financial protection in Croatia’ (2018).
