How many households face financial hardship?

In 2019, 1.5% of households had catastrophic health spending. In the poorest fifth of households it was 4.7%; in the richest fifth, 0.6%. Among households with catastrophic spending, medical products accounted for the largest share of out-of-pocket payments (50.1%). 0.7% of households were impoverished or further impoverished by out-of-pocket payments (2019).

Who goes without care?

In 2018, 4.5% of people had unmet need for health care: 4.9% in the poorest fifth and 3.3% in the richest. In 2018, 2.5% of people had unmet need for dental care: 3.2% in the poorest fifth and 1.4% in the richest. 1.3% of people needing care could not afford a prescribed medicine (2014).

How much do people pay out of pocket?

Out-of-pocket payments were 14.6% of current spending on health in 2024. The median across 53 countries is 22.0%. Households spent 1.6% of their budget on out-of-pocket payments for health care (2019).

How much does government spend on health?

Public spending on health was 9.1% of GDP in 2024. The median across 53 countries is 5.5%. Health took 20.1% of government spending (2023).

Where are the gaps in coverage?

Main gaps in population coverage

Emergency care, primary care and a range of community health services are available free of charge for everyone living in the UK, regardless of residence status. But some people with irregular or insecure immigration status can face barriers to access if they are asked to provide ID or proof of address when registering with a National Health Service (NHS) provider and are incorrectly denied registration. When it comes to hospital care, overseas visitors may be required to pay for treatment, but there are exemptions for certain groups of people, like asylum seekers and refugees. However, a survey of members of the British Medical Association found that 55% of doctors working with refugees and asylum seekers were frequently or sometimes uncertain about their entitlements, which can pose a barrier to access.

Main gaps in service coverage

The National Health Service does not cover eye care (glasses, contact lenses) for working-age adults, unless they have a low income or certain chronic conditions. It covers dental care, but the supply of dental practices offering publicly financed treatment is limited: in 2019/20 more than half of all adults had not seen a dentist within the previous 24 months (as is recommended) and more than two in five children had not had an appointment in the previous 12 months. Substantial waiting times for planned hospital care is also an issue and waiting times tend to be significantly longer in more deprived areas.

Main gaps caused by user charges

Most adults in the UK are required to pay user charges for dental care and eye care (glasses and contact lenses). Adults are also required to pay for outpatient prescriptions in England, but there are exemptions for people aged > 59, people with low incomes and people with selected chronic conditions; this means about 90% of prescriptions are provided without charge.

VHI role in filling gaps in coverage

VHI (commonly known as private medical insurance in the UK) mainly plays a supplementary role, providing access to private providers (e.g. private hospitals and specialists and private wards of NHS hospitals) and faster access to diagnostic tests and treatment. It can also be used to obtain services not well covered by the National Health Service (e.g. dental care), including some that are considered low value, but this depends on each VHI policy and does not apply to experimental tests or treatment. In 2022 it accounted for 2.5% of current spending on health. Expanding VHI is likely to increase inequality in access to health care.
