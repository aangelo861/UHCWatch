How many households face financial hardship?

In 2017, 2.1% of households had catastrophic health spending. In the poorest fifth of households it was 9.2%; in the richest fifth, 0.2%. Among households with catastrophic spending, outpatient care accounted for the largest share of out-of-pocket payments (23.9%). 1.5% of households were impoverished or further impoverished by out-of-pocket payments (2017).

Who goes without care?

In 2024, 4.1% of people had unmet need for health care: 6.3% in the poorest fifth and 2.2% in the richest. In 2024, 7.0% of people had unmet need for dental care: 11.7% in the poorest fifth and 2.6% in the richest. 2.4% of people needing care could not afford a prescribed medicine (2019).

How much do people pay out of pocket?

Out-of-pocket payments were 9.2% of current spending on health in 2024. The median across 53 countries is 22.0%. Households spent 1.8% of their budget on out-of-pocket payments for health care (2017).

How much does government spend on health?

Public spending on health was 9.7% of GDP in 2024. The median across 53 countries is 5.5%. Health took 13.8% of government spending (2024).

Where are the gaps in coverage?

Main gaps in population coverage

Undocumented migrants are excluded from the SHI scheme but can benefit from free access to health care through the AME scheme if they have been in France for at least three months and have an annual income of less than €10 339 (for a single person in 2025).

People living in France for less than three months can access emergency care only.

Main gaps in service coverage

Some types of care are less well-covered by the SHI scheme – particularly dental care and medical products such as glasses and hearing aids. General and specialist outpatient care, dental care and care provided by midwives are subject to retrospective reimbursement by the SHI scheme.

Main gaps caused by user charges

User charges are applied to all types of health care, mainly in the form of percentage co-payments.

There is no cap on percentage co-payments or balance and extra billing. Caps on fixed co-payments are not linked to household income.

Balance billing is permitted for some general practitioners and specialists (in Sector 2), dental care and medical products such as optical care (glasses and contact lenses) and hearing aids; it is also permitted for physician services in SHI-contracted private hospitals.

Extra billing is permitted for inpatient care for non-clinical care (e.g. more comfortable accommodation).

VHI role in filling gaps in coverage

Private complementary health insurance (CHI) plays a substantial role in the French health system, mainly covering co-payments and balance billing for social health insurance benefits. It is mandatory and subsidized by employers for employees in private companies and free or subsidized by the Government for people with low incomes. As a result, CHI covers around 95% of the population and accounted for 12% of current spending on health in 2024 (WHO, 2026). It does not fully address the problems caused by user charges, however: households with the lowest incomes are the least likely to have any form of CHI and CHI is a highly regressive way of financing the health system. It also involves significant transaction and financial costs for the Government and employers.

Source: UHC watch policy explorer.

What does UHC Watch recommend?

UHC Watch’s recommendations for improving financial protection in France, in its own words.

Improve the use of public resources to reduce co-payments, including balance billing

This can be achieved by exempting CSS beneficiaries and people with chronic conditions from all co-payments so that they no longer need Complementary Health Insurance (CHI); and by introducing an annual cap on all co-payments for the whole population and linking it to household income. Other options to reduce financial uncertainty, increase transparency and enhance access include, for example, limiting balance billing for all types of health care, replacing percentage co-payments with low, fixed co-payments and phasing out retrospective reimbursement.

Reduce the regressivity of complementary health insurance (CHI)

This could be achieved by simplifying and automating administrative procedures to prevent households from losing CSS coverage from one year to another; setting monthly contributions at levels that encourage higher take-up among those eligible; reviewing eligibility thresholds to ensure they cover all people at risk of poverty or social exclusion; and linking subsidies for CHI for government and private-sector employees to income so that they are more generous for people with lower incomes.

Improve coverage of dental care and access for underserved groups

The Government can also improve the coverage of dental care, to reduce income inequality in unmet need for this type of care.

Find ways to ensure the whole population is covered

Access to publicly financed health care for undocumented migrants could be improved by simplifying and automating administrative procedures.

Source: 'Can people afford to pay for health care? New evidence on financial protection in France' (2024).
