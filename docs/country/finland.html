How many households face financial hardship?

In 2016, 3.8% of households had catastrophic health spending. In the poorest fifth of households it was 11.8%; in the richest fifth, 0.9%. Among households with catastrophic spending, medicines accounted for the largest share of out-of-pocket payments (30.6%). 1.2% of households were impoverished or further impoverished by out-of-pocket payments (2016).

Who goes without care?

In 2025, 7.8% of people had unmet need for health care: 10.7% in the poorest fifth and 4.6% in the richest. In 2025, 7.5% of people had unmet need for dental care: 9.3% in the poorest fifth and 6.0% in the richest. 8.1% of people needing care could not afford a prescribed medicine (2019).

How much do people pay out of pocket?

Out-of-pocket payments were 14.1% of current spending on health in 2023. The median across 53 countries is 22.0%. Households spent 4.0% of their budget on out-of-pocket payments for health care (2016).

How much does government spend on health?

Public spending on health was 8.5% of GDP in 2023. The median across 53 countries is 5.5%. Health took 15.2% of government spending (2023).

Where are the gaps in coverage?

Main gaps in population coverage

Entitlement to publicly financed health care is based on residence and age, which means all permanent residents and all children < 18 (regardless of legal or coverage status) are automatically covered. However, employed residents also benefit from occupational health care, giving them significantly faster access to primary care than self-employed or unemployed people and people outside the labour force (e.g. children and pensioners).

Adult asylum seekers, undocumented migrants and adults without a municipality of residence lack entitlement to the full range of publicly financed health care. They have access to a limited set of services, often at the discretion of health care professionals, but are required to pay for them out of pocket – although the Government reimburses municipalities for the care of people with low incomes who are unable to pay.

Main gaps in service coverage

The presence of multiple coverage schemes leads to complexity and variation in benefits across groups of people and exacerbates inequality in access to health care.

Although the publicly financed benefits package is relatively comprehensive, coverage of eye care (glasses or contact lenses) and psychotherapy is limited.

Access to most health care (especially gynaecology and dental care) is hindered by waiting times. There are differences in access and quality for health care financed by the wellbeing services counties and occupational health care, reflecting long waiting times for non-urgent health and dental care in public facilities and a shortage of doctors in public facilities due to lower wages compared to private facilities.

Geographical barriers to access due to distance affect people in the northernmost parts of the country and, in the last decade, the closure of public facilities has increased travel times for people in southern parts of the country.

Main gaps caused by user charges

Co-payments are widely applied, are complex in design and vary substantially across wellbeing services counties (WSCs), especially for home care. All children < 18 are automatically exempt from co-payments for WSC health care, but there are no automatic exemptions based on household income; instead, people with low incomes must apply for social assistance to cover co-payments for WSC health care and co-payments for outpatient prescribed medicines unther the National Health Insurance scheme. As a result, take up of social assistance may be low; only 34% of households that reported financial barriers to accessing health care applied for social assistance and only 17% received it. Although there are annual caps on co-payments for health care (outpatient primary, specialist and emergency care), these caps are separate, set at a high level and not linked to household income; in addition, people have to keep track of co-payments themselves to benefit from these caps. Just 7% of the population reach one of the three caps and only 0.2% reach all three caps.

VHI role in filling gaps in coverage

VHI mainly plays a complementary role covering out-of-pocket payments in private facilities and co-payments for publicly financed health care. It also plays a supplementary role providing faster access to treatment in private facilities. People often seek VHI coverage to access specialist care without a referral or to bypass waiting lists in public primary care facilities. VHI covers the use of health care in non-contracted private facilities, mainly to reduce waiting times for non-working people (e.g. children and pensioners) who are unable to benefit from occupational primary care coverage. VHI covers user charges. Take-up of VHI covering user charges is significant, particularly among children: in 2019 12% of adults and 45% of children had VHI.

The share of the population with VHI has increased over time. In 2019 12% of adults and 45% of children had VHI, up from 8% and 37% respectively in 2009. In 2021 it accounted for 1.6% of current spending on health. Expanding VHI is likely to increase inequality in access to health care.
