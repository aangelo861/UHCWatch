How many households face financial hardship?

In 2023, 5.7% of households had catastrophic health spending. In the poorest fifth of households it was 20.8%; in the richest fifth, 2.1%. Among households with catastrophic spending, dental care accounted for the largest share of out-of-pocket payments (44.8%). 3.0% of households were impoverished or further impoverished by out-of-pocket payments (2023).

Who goes without care?

In 2025, 0.3% of people had unmet need for health care: 0.6% in the poorest fifth and 0.2% in the richest. In 2025, 1.0% of people had unmet need for dental care: 2.1% in the poorest fifth and 0.3% in the richest. 3.1% of people needing care could not afford a prescribed medicine (2019).

How much do people pay out of pocket?

Out-of-pocket payments were 14.1% of current spending on health in 2024. The median across 53 countries is 22.0%. Households spent 3.2% of their budget on out-of-pocket payments for health care (2023).

Where are the gaps in coverage?

Main gaps in population coverage

Access to publicly financed health care, including social health insurance (SHI) benefits, is based on permanent residence, so all permanent residents are covered. The people most likely to lack SHI coverage are undocumented migrants, who are not entitled to any publicly financed health care.

Main gaps in service coverage

Coverage of corrective lenses is limited for adults and although dental care is fully covered, it is limited in terms of scope and materials and most dentists are not willing to offer covered procedures or use covered materials. Specialist health care is less accessible outside large cities and there is a shortage of pharmacies and dentists in border areas. Waiting times are an issue in paediatric care. Waiting time targets are in place but are not guaranteed because they are not systematically monitored. Informal payments are not systematically monitored either but international data sources suggest they are higher in Czechia than in most EU countries and typically occur in maternity care to shorten waiting times or see a specific doctor.

Main gaps caused by user charges

User charges are applied to outpatient emergency care (fixed co-payments) and outpatient prescribed medicines and medical products (reference pricing). People with low incomes are exempt from co-payments for emergency care. There are no exemptions from other co-payments but there is an annual cap on some co-payments for outpatient prescribed medicines, which is lower (more protective) for children, older people and people with disabilities. There is no cap on all co-payments. Although extra billing is not permitted, some health care providers charge people for non-clinical services (e.g. scheduling appointments at specific times or sending people text message reminders), particularly in larger cities.

VHI role in filling gaps in coverage

VHI plays a minor substitutive role, covering non-EU nationals who are obliged to buy private health insurance. In 2023 VHI accounted for 0.1% of current spending on health. Expanding VHI is likely to increase inequality in access to health care.

Source: UHC Watch policy explorer.

What does UHC Watch recommend?

UHC Watch’s recommendations for improving financial protection in Czechia, in its own words.

Build on recent efforts to reduce the “avoidable co-payments” caused by reference pricing for outpatient prescribed medicines

This can be done by ensuring that the fully covered medicine in each reference group is available in pharmacies; waiving reference pricing when the fully covered medicine is not available at the local pharmacy; exempting people with low incomes from co-payments currently eligible for the cap or extending the lowest cap (CZK 500) to people with low incomes; finding other ways to link the cap on co-payments to income; closely monitoring “avoidable co-payments” and their causes; further reducing the VAT rate for covered medicines; and improving access to pharmacies in underserved areas.

Reduce out-of-pocket payments for dental care

This can be achieved by requiring dental care providers to offer covered services and materials; expanding the coverage of dental care, including the use of higher-quality materials; and improving access to dental care in underserved areas.

Expand coverage of medical products, especially for people with low incomes

Reduce out-of-pocket payments for outpatient visits

This can be done by removing administrative barriers to the exemption from co-payments for emergency care or abolish this co-payment since it is unlikely to be addressing the root cause of inappropriate use of emergency care; enforcing laws prohibiting extra billing; and taking steps to systematically monitor and address long waiting times and informal payments, which reduce transparency and are likely to be particularly detrimental for people with low incomes.

Address administrative and language barriers that hinder asylum seekers and migrants from accessing entitlements and expand access to publicly financed health care for undocumented migrants

Ensure that public spending on health is able to meet equity and efficiency goals, now and in the future

Public spending on health should be carefully targeted to reduce financial hardship and unmet need for households with low incomes. The Government can take steps to broaden the SHI scheme’s revenue base is broad so that it will generate sufficient funding as the population ages.

Source: ‘Can people afford to pay for health care? New evidence on financial protection in Czechia’ (2025).
