How many households face financial hardship?

In 2020, 7.2% of households had catastrophic health spending. In the poorest fifth of households it was 18.9%; in the richest fifth, 0.4%. Among households with catastrophic spending, medicines accounted for the largest share of out-of-pocket payments (58.6%). 2.2% of households were impoverished or further impoverished by out-of-pocket payments (2020).

Who goes without care?

In 2025, 7.7% of people had unmet need for health care: 8.3% in the poorest fifth and 7.3% in the richest. In 2025, 5.5% of people had unmet need for dental care: 8.9% in the poorest fifth and 3.4% in the richest. 4.1% of people needing care could not afford a prescribed medicine (2019).

How much do people pay out of pocket?

Out-of-pocket payments were 21.4% of current spending on health in 2024. The median across 53 countries is 22.0%. Households spent 4.4% of their budget on out-of-pocket payments for health care (2020).

How much does government spend on health?

Public spending on health was 6.0% of GDP in 2024. The median across 53 countries is 5.5%. Health took 13.0% of government spending (2023).

Where are the gaps in coverage?

Main gaps in population coverage

In 2022 about 4% of people were not covered by the Estonian Health Insurance Fund (EHIF), rising to 10% among people aged 20–29 years. Those most likely to lack coverage are working-age people in part-time or unstable work or people working in the informal economy.

Main gaps in service coverage

The EHIF does not cover optician services and products (e.g. glasses and contact lenses) or over-the-counter medicines. Coverage of dental care is very limited for adults. There are long waiting times for specialist outpatient visits and elective surgery. There is a shortage of health care professionals in primary and specialist care, especially in remote areas.

Main gaps caused by user charges

There are extensive percentage co-payments for outpatient prescribed medicines and heavy percentage co-payments for some inpatient nursing care. There are no explicit exemptions from co-payments for people with low incomes, no cap on co-payments for outpatient prescribed medicines and no overall cap on co-payments. Balance billing takes place for some primary care services. Extra billing is allowed in facilities contracted by the EHIF.

VHI role in filling gaps in coverage

VHI does not play significant role in filling gaps in coverage. Since 2002 the EHIF has provided substitutive VHI for those not eligible for mandatory EHIF coverage (617 people in 2021). Supplementary and complementary VHI plays a small but growing role, providing faster access to covered services and some services not covered by the EHIF. VHI accounted for 0.3% of current spending on health in 2021 and covered 7% of the working-age population in 2022. The tax subsidies for VHI introduced in 2018 are likely to increase the number of people with VHI and exacerbate inequality in access to health care.

Source: UHC watch policy explorer.

What does UHC Watch recommend?

UHC Watch’s recommendations for improving financial protection in Estonia, in its own words.

Change the basis for entitlement to residence

The gap in population coverage should be closed by changing the basis for entitlement to Estonian Health Insurance Fund (EHIF) benefits to residence.

Increase and fine-tune benefits to better target those in most need

Dental care and other benefits should be increased and fine-tuned so that they are better targeted to help households in most need.

Protection from co-payments should be increased for households with low incomes – for example, by setting a cap for them or exempting them from all co-payments.

Monitor and reduce co-payments

Co-payments should be monitored in a more person-centred way (that is, looking at co-payments across all types of health care financed by the EHIF) and a cap on all co-payments should be introduced – for example, by extending the protection mechanism used for outpatient prescribed medicines and applying it to all services.

Out-of-pocket payments for long-term health care can be reduced by abolishing percentage co-payments and establishing a cap – ideally one that applies to co-payments for all types of health care.

Balance billing for primary care services (such as those provided by psychologists, physiotherapists and speech therapists) should be abolished to ensure that access does not depend on ability to pay.

Continue to improve medicines policy

Out-of-pocket payments should continue to be reduced for prescribed medicines and medical products by ensuring that health care providers and pharmacies have incentives to prescribe and dispense the cheapest alternatives and through price regulation.

Source: ‘Can people afford to pay for health care? New evidence on financial protection in Estonia' (2023).
