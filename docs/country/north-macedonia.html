How many households face financial hardship?

In 2024, 9.3% of households had catastrophic health spending. In the poorest fifth of households it was 22.6%; in the richest fifth, 5.4%. Among households with catastrophic spending, medicines accounted for the largest share of out-of-pocket payments (74.9%). 5.3% of households were impoverished or further impoverished by out-of-pocket payments (2024).

Who goes without care?

How much do people pay out of pocket?

Out-of-pocket payments were 39.6% of current spending on health in 2023. The median across 53 countries is 22.0%. Households spent 3.7% of their budget on out-of-pocket payments for health care (2024).

How much does government spend on health?

Public spending on health was 4.3% of GDP in 2023. The median across 53 countries is 5.5%. Health took 12.1% of government spending (2023).

Where are the gaps in coverage?

Main gaps in population coverage

Given that the basis for entitlement is linked to payment of contributions, people without regular employment, self-employed people who have not paid contributions and people whose employers delay paying wages for more than two months due to liquidity problems are at risk of not being covered. Undocumented migrants and others who are not covered are only entitled to publicly financed emergency care.

Main gaps in service coverage

The positive list for outpatient prescribed medicines is narrow in scope and not updated regularly. Public hospitals are characterised by poor service quality and infrastructure. Informal payments are common in gynaecology in primary care.

Main gaps caused by user charges

User charges are applied to most health services and are in the form of percentage co-payments ranging from 0% to 20% of the reference price. People with low incomes are not exempt from co-payments for outpatient medicines and medical products.

VHI role in filling gaps in coverage

Supplementary VHI offers people access to non-contracted private facilities and use of private rooms and access to services outside working hours in public facilities. Complementary VHI covers co-payments for publicly financed health care but take-up is extremely low. The number of VHI contracts was around 1.8% of the population in 2023 but contracts typically cover dependants so the actual share of the population covered by VHI is slightly higher than 1.8% (ISA, 2024). In 2022 VHI accounted for 2.6% of current spending on health (WHO, 2025). Expanding VHI is likely to increase inequality in access to health care.

What does UHC Watch recommend?

UHC Watch’s recommendations for improving financial protection in North Macedonia, in its own words.

Further expand the coverage and accessibility of cost-effective outpatient medicines

Building on the recent expansion of the positive list of outpatient medicines, the Health Insurance Fund (HIF) should give priority to further expanding the list; align the list with national clinical guidelines; relax prescribing rules so that common chronic conditions can be treated by family doctors; and ensure that cost-effective medicines and diagnostic tests are available in primary care, starting with those listed in national clinical guidelines.

Improve efficiency and equity in the use of medicines

The HIF and the Ministry of Health should continue to work with health care providers and pharmacies to ensure appropriate prescribing and dispensing and encourage greater use of generic medicines. Options include systematically monitoring prescribing and dispensing, starting with the universal enforcement of electronic prescribing; making generic substitution in pharmacies mandatory, so that people always benefit from the lowest-priced alternative; strengthening regulation to limit the influence of pharmaceutical companies on prescribing and dispensing behaviour; and monitoring the prevalence of different types of co-payments and protection from co-payments using existing digital tools.

Strengthen co-payment design to protect people with low incomes from all co-payments

This could be done by exempting the cost-effective outpatient medicines and diagnostic tests included in the national clinical guidelines from co-payments; exempting households with low incomes from all co-payments; extending the annual cap on co-payments to all co-payments; and where feasible, replacing percentage co-payments with low, fixed co-payments.

Reduce the “avoidable co-payments” caused by reference pricing, which account for around 40% of co-payments for outpatient medicines

This could be done by ensuring that at least one medicine in each reference group is available at the reference price; waiving reference pricing when higher-priced alternatives are prescribed for therapeutic reasons; aligning reference prices and maximum retail prices; and monitoring the availability of medicines at the reference price in pharmacies.

Abolish balance billing

Balance billing reduces transparency and financial certainty for people accessing health care. Very few countries in Europe use balance billing and those that do are trying to eliminate it.

Monitor and reduce waiting times and informal payments and control the use of private care in public and private facilities

Collecting administrative data and, where necessary, survey data, would help to assess the magnitude of waiting times, informal payments and the use of and interaction between private care in public and private facilities; increase understanding of their effect on households and the health system; and identify strategies to curb any negative impact on efficiency and equity in the use of limited financial and human resources for health.

Improve the collection of health spending data in the household budget survey by aligning methods with international standards

This could be done by extending the recall period for less frequent but high-cost types of care (e.g. inpatient care) and improving the disaggregation level of data on household spending on health to allow better estimation of the main drivers of financial hardship.

Ensure universal and permanent population coverage for adults and children

Options include (i) ensuring that people (and their dependants) do not lose HIF coverage when they experience a change in their work circumstances or employers fail to pay wages on time; and (ii) changing the basis for entitlement to HIF benefits from payment of mandatory health insurance contributions to residence (as in other countries in Europe with social health insurance schemes). This change would ensure that all residents are entitled to the same health care benefits, throughout the life course, and that non-payment of mandatory contributions are treated in the same way as non-payment of other taxes (e.g. using fines, when relevant, rather than denying people access to health care).

Broaden the public revenue base for the health system to reduce reliance on employment-based contributions (payroll taxes), so that public revenue for health does not shrink as the population ages

Options include (i) making greater use of government budget transfers to the HIF; and (ii) extending the levy base for some or all of the mandatory health insurance contribution to non-wage forms of income.

Support these efforts by increasing the priority given to health in allocating the government budget and ensuring that any additional public investment is carefully used to meet equity and efficiency goals

Source: ‘Can people afford to pay for health care? New evidence on financial protection in North Macedonia' (2025).
