How many households face financial hardship?

In 2020, 5.2% of households had catastrophic health spending. In the poorest fifth of households it was 12.2%; in the richest fifth, 5.0%. Among households with catastrophic spending, medical products accounted for the largest share of out-of-pocket payments (39.7%). 1.1% of households were impoverished or further impoverished by out-of-pocket payments (2020).

Who goes without care?

How much do people pay out of pocket?

Out-of-pocket payments were 21.5% of current spending on health in 2023. The median across 53 countries is 22.0%. Households spent 6.3% of their budget on out-of-pocket payments for health care (2020).

Public spending on health was 8.0% of GDP in 2023. The median across 53 countries is 5.5%. Health took 14.9% of government spending (2023).

Where are the gaps in coverage?

Main gaps in population coverage

In 2022 about 1% of the population was not covered (uninsured), rising to 1.3% in Brussels and 2% among younger adults (20–39 years).

Lack of access to social health insurance coverage for asylum seekers, undocumented migrants, posted workers (people sent by their employer to carry out a service in another EU Member State on a temporary basis), homeless people and people who cannot afford to pay contributions.

People whose social contributions are not automatically collected face complex coverage rules."

Main gaps in service coverage

Social health insurance (SHI) coverage of dental care and medical products (e.g. hearing aids and optical care such as glasses and contact lenses) is limited and SHI does not cover over-the-counter medicines.

Although mandatory complementary health insurance (CHI) provided by sickness funds covers services like orthodontics and optical care (glasses and contact lenses), it is financed through flat-rate contributions, which are regressive.

Benefits in kind (third-party payment) do not automatically apply to all outpatient care, only to inpatient care and outpatient medicines.

Main gaps caused by user charges

Complex co-payments are applied to almost all health services, including primary care visits. There are very few exemptions from co-payments (e.g. for dental care for children < 19 and general practitioner and outpatient specialist care for children and young adults < 25 eligible for ‘increased reimbursement’ and with a global medical record) and no general exemption for people with low incomes. Reduced co-payments are not automatically granted to low-income households and there are major gaps in take-up. Balance billing is widespread in hospitals (‘fee supplements’) and in outpatient care. Co-payment protection mechanisms do not apply to balance billing. Most outpatient care is subject to retrospective reimbursement.

VHI role in filling gaps in coverage

VHI plays a mixed complementary and supplementary role. It initially focused on offering subscribers a fixed payment a day in hospital, but over time coverage has been extended to co-payments (and other user charges) for hospital stays to account for increases in balance billing (fee supplements). VHI can also cover dental care and ambulatory services, including balance billing and other co-payments, but this part of the market is relatively small. Although VHI is estimated to cover quite a large share of the population, richer households are much more likely to be covered than households with lower incomes. In 2022 it accounted for 4.7% of current spending on health. Expanding VHI is likely to increase inequality in access to health care.

Source: UHC Watch policy explorer.

What does UHC Watch recommend?

UHC Watch’s recommendations for improving financial protection in Belgium, in its own words.

Ensure the social health insurance (SHI) scheme covers the whole population

The Government should find ways to ensure that the whole population is covered.

Continue to strengthen co-payment policy so that it is better able to protect households with low incomes

Grant automatic entitlement to everyone eligible for reduced co-payments (increased reimbursement) to eliminate administrative barriers to take-up.

Find a way to reduce co-payments for households with low incomes – for example, by exempting them from co-payments, further lowering the annual cap on co-payments (maximum billing [maximumfactuur-maximum a facturer], MAF) for them or introducing an additional lower cap targeted at households with very low incomes.

Raise awareness of the cap by informing people about it at the beginning of each year instead of waiting until they cross the spending threshold.

Extend the cap to user charges for prescribed non-covered medicines and long-term psychiatric care.

Abolish retrospective reimbursement for all health services covered by the SHI scheme

This includes abolishing all up-front payment of co-payments above the cap (MAF).

Continue to limit balance billing in outpatient and inpatient care

Further regulate and reform the way in which hospitals are financed. Increase the share of ‘conventioned’ practitioners who apply the official tariff and abolish partial ‘conventioning’.

Reduce out-of-pocket payments for medical products

Further strengthen regulation of the price of non-covered medical products such as hearing aids, glasses and contact lenses.

Source: UHC Watch policy explorer and ‘Can people afford to pay for health care? New evidence on financial protection in Belgium’ (2023).
