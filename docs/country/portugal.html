How many households face financial hardship?

In 2022, 7.5% of households had catastrophic health spending. In the poorest fifth of households it was 29.0%; in the richest fifth, 0.6%. Among households with catastrophic spending, medicines accounted for the largest share of out-of-pocket payments (42.4%). 3.3% of households were impoverished or further impoverished by out-of-pocket payments (2022).

Who goes without care?

In 2025, 2.5% of people had unmet need for health care: 4.2% in the poorest fifth and 1.0% in the richest. In 2025, 7.9% of people had unmet need for dental care: 16.9% in the poorest fifth and 1.6% in the richest. 6.7% of people needing care could not afford a prescribed medicine (2019).

How much do people pay out of pocket?

Out-of-pocket payments were 28.2% of current spending on health in 2024. The median across 53 countries is 22.0%. Households spent 5.3% of their budget on out-of-pocket payments for health care (2022).

How much does government spend on health?

Public spending on health was 6.4% of GDP in 2024. The median across 53 countries is 5.5%. Health took 14.6% of government spending (2023).

Where are the gaps in coverage?

Main gaps in population coverage

Entitlement is based on residence, so there are no gaps in population coverage for permanent residents. Refugees, asylum seekers, the children of undocumented migrants and some undocumented migrant adults are entitled to the same benefits as residents; however, administrative barriers may hinder access to health care for these groups of people.

Main gaps in service coverage

Access to dental care is restricted due to the lack of sufficient public facilities. Corrective lenses are limited to people over 65 years with low incomes receiving social support only.

Waiting times are an issue, particularly for outpatient specialist visits and elective surgery and partly due to a shortage of health professionals. Waiting time targets are often exceeded. As a result, people with high incomes resort to treatment in the private sector.

The archipelagos experience access challenges, especially in the Azores; not all islands have hospitals and transport can be affected by weather conditions.

Main gaps caused by user charges

User charges are applied to most types of care except primary care visits, diagnostic tests in primary care and inpatient care. Percentage co-payments of up to 85% are applied to outpatient prescribed medicines and medical products. Very few people with low incomes (older people receiving social support only) are exempt from co-payments for outpatient prescribed medicines, there are no exemptions from co-payments for medical products and existing exemptions are not applied automatically. There is no cap on co-payments. People may pay more than expected out-of-pocket for covered medicines as the lowest-priced alternatives are not always available in pharmacies.

VHI role in filling gaps in coverage

VHI plays a supplementary role providing people with faster access to health care or increased choice of provider and is provided by two types of entities: so called subsystems and commercial insurance companies. In 2023 31% of the population had some form of VHI and subsystem and commercial VHI together accounted for 7.6% of current spending on health in 2023. Expanding VHI is likely to increase inequality in access to health care.

Source: UHC Watch policy explorer.

What does UHC Watch recommend?

UHC Watch’s recommendations for improving financial protection in Portugal, in its own words.

Enhance access to outpatient prescribed medicines as part of primary care

Options include replacing percentage co-payments with low fixed co-payments (which are more transparent); ensuring people can access at least one of the three lowest-priced medicines in each reference group free at the point of use; monitoring prescribing patterns, including the role of generic medicines; making generic substitution mandatory in pharmacies (but allowing users to opt for a more expensive alternative if they prefer); introducing penalties for pharmacies that are not able to provide people immediately with the lowest-priced medicine; and avoiding further increases in medicine prices.

Improve mechanisms to protect people with low incomes from co-payments, especially co-payments for outpatient prescribed medicines

Extend exemptions from outpatient prescribed medicines to all people with low incomes, not just pensioners; introduce a cap on all co-payments and link the cap to household income; and make sure exemptions and caps are applied automatically, using digital tools, for everyone.

Continue with efforts to reduce waiting times and improve access to primary care

Closely monitor waiting times to make sure targets are met. Facilitate access to NHS family doctors by ensuring sufficient staff numbers and enhancing the role of other health professionals in providing care (e.g. nurses, dentists, psychologists and social workers) to increase access to primary care.

Expand access to publicly financed dental care

Options include extending the number of municipalities in the pilot project on access to dental care in NHS primary care centres; implementing planned investment in dedicated offices for dental care in primary care; selectively contracting private providers where needed; and simplifying the dental care voucher system – for example, by extending vouchers to all people with low incomes, without time or volume limits on care.

Expand coverage of medical products, especially for people with low incomes

Add corrective lenses (e.g. glasses) to the benefits package and reduce heavy user charges for covered medical products (currently percentage co-payments ranging from 15% to 85% of the price), particularly for people with low incomes.

Evaluate the benefits and costs of tax subsidies for out-of-pocket payments and VHI premiums

These tax subsidies typically favour richer households and shift public resources away from health need. Public resources are more likely to meet equity and efficiency goals if they are used to reduce financial hardship and unmet need for households with low incomes.

Source: ‘Can people afford to pay for health care? New evidence on financial protection in Portugal’ (2025).
