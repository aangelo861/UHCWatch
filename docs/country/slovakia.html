How many households face financial hardship?

In 2023, 4.4% of households had catastrophic health spending. In the poorest fifth of households it was 20.9%; in the richest fifth, 0.2%. Among households with catastrophic spending, medicines accounted for the largest share of out-of-pocket payments (55.8%). 3.3% of households were impoverished or further impoverished by out-of-pocket payments (2023).

Who goes without care?

In 2024, 1.6% of people had unmet need for health care: 1.4% in the poorest fifth and 1.4% in the richest. In 2024, 1.4% of people had unmet need for dental care: 3.1% in the poorest fifth and 0.6% in the richest. 3.0% of people needing care could not afford a prescribed medicine (2019).

How much do people pay out of pocket?

Out-of-pocket payments were 20.1% of current spending on health in 2023. The median across 53 countries is 22.0%. Households spent 2.4% of their budget on out-of-pocket payments for health care (2023).

Public spending on health was 5.8% of GDP in 2023. The median across 53 countries is 5.5%. Health took 12.1% of government spending (2023).

Where are the gaps in coverage?

Main gaps in population coverage

The basis for entitlement to SHI benefits is payment of mandatory contributions by people or by the Government on behalf of selected groups. In 2022 around 5% of the eligible population (246 058 people) was not covered by the SHI scheme – mainly people working in other EU countries and homeless people.

People who fail to pay their contributions for three months, homeless people, asylum seekers who are unemployed and other people not eligible for SHI or not covered by the SHI scheme have access only to publicly financed emergency care, maternity care and treatment of communicable diseases.

Undocumented migrants are not entitled to any publicly financed health care.

Main gaps in service coverage

Coverage of dental care and glasses is limited. Waiting times are an issue for outpatient care. Informal payments are also widespread in outpatient care, mainly because health care providers charge people for services such as a first visit to a clinic, setting up a patient file or ambulance running costs. Roma people living in segregated settlements in eastern Slovakia experience additional access barriers.

Main gaps caused by user charges

User charges are applied to emergency care, outpatient prescribed medicines, medical products and dental care treatment, usually in the form of fixed co-payments. There are protection mechanisms in place, but these are quite narrow: reduced co-payments only apply to emergency care, while exemptions and caps on co-payments for outpatient prescribed medicines and medical products only apply to children under 6 years old, pensioners with low incomes and people who have a disability with low incomes.

Extra billing for non-clinical services occurs in outpatient and inpatient care settings.

VHI role in filling gaps in coverage

VHI plays an extremely small supplementary role, covering care in case of accident or illness, medical costs incurred abroad or the cost of mountain rescue in emergency cases. In 2015 0.6% of the population had VHI. No information is available on the VHI share of current spending on health. Expanding VHI is likely to increase inequality in access to health care.

Source: UHC Watch policy explorer.

What does UHC Watch recommend?

UHC Watch’s recommendations for improving financial protection in Slovakia, in its own words.

Ensure people have access to outpatient prescribed medicines without co-payments

This can be achieved by including at least one medicine with a €0 co-payment medicine in every reference group; guaranteeing the availability of this medicine in pharmacies throughout the country; strengthening adherence to clinical guidelines and INN prescribing by doctors, as well as generic substitution in pharmacies; and simplifying the system of co-payments for outpatient prescribed medicines to a single co-payment for all medicines incurring co-payments over €1 – this would also enhance transparency, equity and efficiency.

Improve the mechanisms used to protect people from co-payments by extending exemptions and caps to all co-payments, to all people with low incomes and, ideally, to people with chronic conditions

These mechanisms should be applied automatically, to ensure everyone eligible benefits from them, and accompanied by regular monitoring to ensure their scope is appropriate.

Reassess the usefulness of co-payments for emergency care by tackling the root causes of overuse of emergency care

Although co-payments for emergency care are in the form of relatively low and transparent fixed co-payments, and some people are exempt in certain circumstances, they are unlikely to address the real reasons why people use emergency services – that is, because of a lack of sufficient access to primary care (including treatment in primary care settings).

Expand publicly financed coverage of dental care

This can be done by covering the use of higher-quality materials and finding ways to ensure that more providers offer covered services.

Tackle inefficiencies in the health system that push people to pay out of pocket for covered care

This can be done by systematically monitoring and enforcing waiting time targets (e.g. those introduced for elective surgery from 2025); closely monitoring the causes and effects of informal payments by establishing regular surveillance and reporting mechanisms; and closing loopholes in the law that enable providers to extra bill people in outpatient and inpatient care settings.

Soften the link between entitlement to SHI benefits and payment of mandatory SHI contributions. This means stopping penalizing people who are unable to pay contributions by denying them access to health care.

Expand the scope of publicly financed health care that is available to people who are not eligible to be covered by the SHI scheme.

Broaden the public revenue base so that is it able to generate sufficient funding as the population ages.

Soften the link between entitlement to SHI benefits and payment of mandatory SHI contributions

This can be achieved by stopping penalizing people who are not able to pay contributions by denying them access to health care, as in Czechia where people continue to have full access to HIIS benefits, even if they have not paid mandatory contributions.

Expand the scope of publicly financed health care that is available to people who are not eligible for or not covered by the SHI scheme

Broaden the public revenue base so that is it able to generate sufficient funding as the population ages and meet equity and efficiency goals, now and in the future

Source: ‘Can people afford to pay for health care? New evidence on financial protection in Slovakia’ (2025).
