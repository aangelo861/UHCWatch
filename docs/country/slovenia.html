How many households face financial hardship?

In 2022, 1.6% of households had catastrophic health spending. In the poorest fifth of households it was 3.4%; in the richest fifth, 1.4%. Among households with catastrophic spending, dental care accounted for the largest share of out-of-pocket payments (63.6%). 0.2% of households were impoverished or further impoverished by out-of-pocket payments (2022).

Who goes without care?

In 2025, 3.2% of people had unmet need for health care: 3.3% in the poorest fifth and 3.2% in the richest. In 2025, 3.9% of people had unmet need for dental care: 4.0% in the poorest fifth and 4.1% in the richest. 4.8% of people needing care could not afford a prescribed medicine (2019).

How much do people pay out of pocket?

Out-of-pocket payments were 11.7% of current spending on health in 2024. The median across 53 countries is 22.0%. Households spent 3.3% of their budget on out-of-pocket payments for health care (2022).

How much does government spend on health?

Public spending on health was 8.6% of GDP in 2024. The median across 53 countries is 5.5%. Health took 14.6% of government spending (2023).

Where are the gaps in coverage?

Main gaps in population coverage

Entitlement to the Health Insurance Institute of Slovenia (HIIS) benefits is based on legal residence and payment of mandatory contributions to the HIIS. Undocumented migrants are not eligible for HIIS coverage. At the end of 2024 about 0.6% of the eligible population was not fully entitled to HIIS benefits; 0.5% due to non-payment of contributions (mainly self-employed people) and 0.1% due to administrative barriers (e.g. people waiting to be recognized as pensioners or unemployed).

Although everyone in Slovenia, regardless of residence status or payment of contributions, is entitled to publicly financed emergency care and a few other services (e.g. selected outpatient medicines, selected dental care, pregnancy and childbirth care, contraception and termination of pregnancy) they must pay for all other care out of pocket. People who are eligible for HIIS coverage but have not paid the required contributions can request retrospective reimbursement of out-of-pocket payments incurred for HIIS benefits care once they have settled their debt to the HIIS.

Main gaps in service coverage

Although the HIIS benefits package is generally comprehensive, there are limitations in the coverage of dental care for adults (which is gradually being expanded) and medical products for visual impairment. Access to covered medical products (corrective lenses and hearing aids) may be hindered by limited public awareness of entitlements and misleading advertising by providers.

Waiting times are an issue for outpatient specialist services (including dental care), inpatient care (mainly elective surgery) and some diagnostic tests. They are the main reason for self-reported unmet need for health and dental care and have grown over time.

Main gaps caused by user charges

Reference pricing is applied to outpatient prescribed medicines and medical products and there are no mechanisms to protect people from these “avoidable co-payments”.

Providers can charge covered people extra for non-standard dental treatment for selected procedures (e.g. prostheses and fillings). A few treatments and groups of people are exempt from some of these charges.

VHI role in filling gaps in coverage

VHI does not generally fill gaps in population coverage. VHI offers faster access to health care for diagnostic tests and other specialist care with long waiting times and can cover extra charges in dental care, but but take up is concentrated in people with higher incomes.

What does UHC Watch recommend?

UHC Watch’s recommendations for improving financial protection in Slovenia, in its own words.

Broaden the public revenue base for the health system to reduce reliance on employment, so that public revenue for health does not shrink as the population ages

Options include greater use of government budget transfers to the Social Health Insurance (SHI) scheme and extending the levy base for some or all of the SHI contribution to non-wage forms of income.

Continue to address long waiting times

The Government should find ways to bring down waiting times to more acceptable levels and ensure that voluntary health insurance (VHI) offering faster access to treatment does not exacerbate inequalities in access to health care.

Protect people with low incomes from any remaining user charges for covered health care

This can be done by exempting people with low incomes from co-payments arising from reference pricing for outpatient prescribed medicines and from extra charges for dental treatment; introducing a cap on all co-payments, ideally linked to income to soften the impact of the cap on the health budget; and applying these protection mechanisms automatically, using digital tools, to ensure all eligible people benefit.

Avoid introducing or increasing user charges in the future

A major reform introduced in 2024 abolished most co-payments – an example of good practice to other countries in Europe and beyond. A large body of evidence shows that user charges are not an effective way of directing people to use health care more efficiently and have negative effects on affordable access to health care and other aspects of health system performance.

Improve the affordability of outpatient medicines, as well as dental care for adults, corrective lenses and hearing aids

Around 13% of all outpatients prescribed medicines dispensed incur “avoidable co- payments” arising from reference pricing. This share could be reduced by ensuring that health care workers have incentives to prescribe and dispense the cheapest alternatives. It may also be useful to explore why households are spending on over-the-counter (OTC) medicines. Ensure affordable access to adult dental care, corrective lenses and hearing aids by granting standard-quality benefits and promoting proper awareness of entitlements among the public and providers.

Address the regressivity of the new flat-rate contribution, so that it no longer imposes a much heavier financial burden on people with lower incomes

The 2024 reform replaced premiums for VHI covering SHI co-payments with a new mandatory flat-rate contribution per person (the Obvezni zdravstveni prispevek, OZP, in Slovenian). Although some groups of people are exempt from the OZP, it is a regressive payment, placing a much heavier financial burden on households with lower incomes.

Find ways to ensure the whole population is covered

Options include changing the basis for entitlement to residence (as France did in 2000) or continuing to give people full access to SHI benefits, even if they have not paid mandatory contributions (as in Czechia). This is particularly important after the 2024 reform, as now entitlement to publicly financed health care depends not just on payment of regular SHI contributions but also on payment of the new mandatory flat-rate contribution (the OZP).

Source: ‘Can people afford to pay for health care? New evidence on financial protection in Slovenia' (2025). See the coverage policy checklist for country examples of good practice in improving financial protection.
